Why so much? AI runs on specialized chips packed into warehouse-sized buildings — data centers. The computing used to train AI has been growing about 5x a year, while chips only get ~1.5x better per dollar each year (Epoch AI). The gap gets filled with more machines — and every machine draws power.

THE GRID

The electricity grid repriced.

PJM runs the power market for more than 67 million people across 13 eastern states and DC. It pays power plants to be ready when needed. For seventeen years that readiness price swung between $16 and $174 — then it rose 9.3x in a single auction, and a price cap has bound the three auctions since, the last two coming up short of the reliability target. Utilities pass wholesale costs into bills — estimates for the first repriced year ran 1.5–5% (PJM figures via WHYY). Wisconsin and most of mid-America are on other grids, where this exact price doesn't apply.

The standby price in dollars per megawatt-day, zero-based scale, 22 delivery years. $269.92/MW-day works out to about 1.1¢ per kWh if spread over flat, round-the-clock use (it was 0.12¢) — your bill's share depends on your region and when you use power. The capped price understates scarcity, and this series alone doesn't prove what caused the jump — PJM has pointed to data-center demand growth in its own commentary (via press). If a data center is coming to your town — six questions to ask the developer

  1. How many megawatts are you requesting from the utility — and is that in the grid operator's queue yet?
  2. Who pays for the new substation and lines — the project, or everyone's rates?
  3. Where does the cooling water come from, and how many gallons a day at peak?
  4. How many years of tax abatement are you asking for, and what's the county's payback math?
  5. How many permanent jobs are written into the agreement — not construction jobs?
  6. If the capacity price rises (see the grid section above), whose bills carry it?
